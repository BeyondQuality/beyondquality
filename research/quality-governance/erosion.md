# The erosion thesis

Part of [Governance of quality](quality-governance.md).

Between the governing body and product, engineering, QA and the departments that own the business numbers runs the interface described on [interface.md](interface.md). The top publishes the six-part statement ([value.md](value.md)). Management decides the everyday cases inside its bounds, five kinds of reports come back up, and at each reevaluation, alongside the refreshed Part 2 trends, the top corrects the numbers and remakes the bets. People do every part of this, and they do it on two kinds of knowledge about the company's software: how it works, and why it is the way it is. [implementations.md](implementations.md) describes what coding agents do to that knowledge: comprehension debt for the how, intent debt for the why.

This page is about what those losses do to the governance system itself, and about who has to mandate and pay for the countermeasures. The interface keeps the statement's numbers current. Keeping the people able to run the interface is the other half of the "maintain" the research promises in its [purpose statement](quality-governance.md).

## What the interface runs on

[implementations.md](implementations.md) follows the two debts through the four-step loop of [Economics of Testing](../testing_economics/testing_economics.md): in step 1 the managers can no longer say what a failure would cost, and in step 3 QA can no longer say whether a check is credible evidence. Without a rule against it, the reports carry the same loss to the top: a risk size built on tests generated from code nobody has read rests on nothing, yet reaches the top in the same units as every well-supported number, so the top cannot tell the two apart. Under the statement QA marks such risks as unsupported ([interface.md](interface.md), report 2), and the assurance check traces a sample of reported numbers back to the raw material behind them; both the marking and the check still need a person who can read that material and tell an independent check from a self-confirming one.

The decision rule on [interface.md](interface.md) presupposes the same people. Under the statement a team facing "ship now or test more" checks the risk against the red lines and, when it fits inside them, works the trade-off out from the published numbers and decides alone. Reinertsen's Boeing 777 example makes the same point: any designer was authorized to increase unit cost by up to $300 to save a pound of weight, and five thousand engineers made system-level trade-offs without asking their superiors for permission ([*The Principles of Product Development Flow*](quality-governance.md#references)). The rule spared the designers the asking, and it did not spare them the judging: each still had to know what their change did to weight and to cost. A published statement spares the team the meeting, and it still needs a team that can say what a failure would cost and whether the checks would catch it.

The value work runs on the same knowledge. The value model on [value.md](value.md) is a set of decisions: which needs get a bet, what level to hold, what holding it is worth, and the top remakes those decisions at each reevaluation. Intent debt shows itself when decisions need to be made ([research question](../ai-era-testing/analysis-research-question.md)), and a reevaluation is a set of exactly such decisions. When the top asks whether the fraud rule in the refund flow still protects anything worth its cost, the answer has to come from someone who knows why the rule is there. Once that intent is gone ([implementations.md](implementations.md)), a team that can describe every line of the code still cannot answer: the bet on that need cannot be checked, and a proposal to retire the rule cannot be judged.

## A loss of a different kind

The managers handle a new risk in the ordinary way. When the company adds a second payment provider, say, they write the new risk down in step 1, rank it in step 2, QA covers it in step 3, and its size appears in the next report. The register grows by a row and the work goes on as before. The two debts are a loss in the people who do that work. [implementations.md](implementations.md) reads intent debt as the wearing away of direction and comprehension debt as the wearing away of oversight, with accountability staying where it is. The AI standard's note on oversight says why that is a loss of a different kind: oversight cannot work without a general grasp of the situation (38507 3.2.1, Note 1). The body's oversight of AI is to rest on a general understanding of how the system is used (6.2). A body whose people no longer understand the software has the same register as before and fewer and fewer people who can tell it what the rows mean, while it still answers for the whole. Its question gains a second half: what are our risks, and can our organization still know them.

The people inside the work cannot see this loss from where they stand, for three reasons.

- Nothing in this quarter's reports shows it. Everyone sees the speed at once: the feature shipped, the build arrived. The debts appear later and elsewhere: diagnosis takes longer, rework grows, a regulation arrives and nobody can say whether the system fits it. The cost appears in engineering's numbers months after the delegation that caused it, and on another team's budget.
- The people cannot feel it. The research cites a study in which experienced developers using AI on their own repositories took 19% longer while believing they were 20% faster ([with agents](../ai-era-testing/analysis-agents.md)). The standard has the general form: staff come to assume a sophisticated AI system is error-free (38507 6.6.1). Asking the developers whether they are losing understanding gives the body the wrong answer.
- The people who could stop it lose the ability as it grows. [implementations.md](implementations.md) describes the circle at the size of one step: judging a bigger step takes understanding nobody is building, accepting it takes nothing, so the steps grow. At the size of a company the same circle runs for years before anyone reports it, because from inside, every turn of it looks like a team getting faster.

## Two ways a product gets worse

Products get worse in two ways, and the debts belong to the second. A company can decide to lower a quality level, to save the money or because its customers cannot leave. Under the statement that is a bet that lowers a level, with an owner, a return and a review date, so the company at least knows what it chose ([value.md](value.md)). Or nobody decides: a thousand small trade-offs go the same way under deadline pressure, each too small to escalate, and the product decays without a decision anyone could point to. The [problem statement](quality-governance.md)'s first two observations describe that second way one decision at a time. Agents add a trade-off of the same kind, made by every developer every day: how much of the work to hand to the agent in one piece. The tools are sold on what one prompt can build and the public benchmarks score the same thing, so the default is set outside the company, and inside it nobody has to decide anything for the debts to grow.

## The floor under the recoverable debt

[implementations.md](implementations.md) distinguishes recoverable comprehension debt from compounding intent debt, and notes the standard's warning about "agent atrophy", skills hollowing out when AI takes over routine work (38507 6.7.4). Agent atrophy is the floor under comprehension debt. Buying comprehension back presupposes someone who can still read the code well enough to supervise, intervene and evolve it ([research question](../ai-era-testing/analysis-research-question.md)). While that skill lasts, the debt can be paid off at a cost. Once the skill has gone, nobody can pay that cost, and the recoverable half of the loss stops being recoverable. Intent debt has no such floor, because the why was never written down and no amount of reading brings it back.

## The tool changes underneath

Because the agent changes on the vendor's schedule ([implementations.md](implementations.md)), assurance over the tool has to be repeated whenever it updates; adaptive AI systems can change the appropriateness of the assurance mechanisms in place, and more than one mechanism may be needed (38500 7.2.7). One mechanism management can keep in its own hands is a suite of checks (calibrated evals, in engineers' terms) its people write and maintain, run against the agent whenever anything in the tooling changes: the same instructions, the same expected results, written by a person who knows what a right result looks like. The suite tells the company whether the tool still behaves as it did, and gives the body a number it can watch over time. Three limits go with it. The expected results have to come from people, because a suite whose answers a model wrote passes while proving nothing, like tests generated from the code they test ([lifecycle drift](../ai-era-testing/analysis-lifecycle.md)). The suite covers the tasks somebody thought to put in it, so a new kind of work is outside it. And what counts as a right result changes with the product, so the suite decays unless people keep it. Keeping it is a prevention cost like the rest: a body of intent written by people, in test form.

The suite also measures the people. In a step-by-step workflow such as the building sessions [implementations.md](implementations.md) describes as a candidate countermeasure, the team accepts each of the agent's steps or sends it back, and how often it sends steps back can be counted. Suppose that over a year the team sends back one step in twenty in its live sessions where it used to send back one in five. Either the agent has got better, or the people have got worse at telling a bad step from a good one, and the session count alone does not say which. The suite separates the two. Its expected results were written by people at a time when they could still tell, and they do not move when the people's judgment drifts. Suppose the suite includes the refund rule (for a $600 refund, the request must wait for a manager) alongside other rules of the same kind, and when run against the current agent the suite still fails roughly one task in five while the team now rejects only one step in twenty in its sessions. The tool did not get four times better; the people stopped catching faults of the kind the suite still catches. The suite has measured the people as well as the tool. No other instrument on this page reads the erosion that directly, and this one works only while someone can still write the expected results.

## The size of the risk to knowledge

Under the statement the risk of losing this knowledge is one kind of risk among the others. The top sets an appetite for it, management reports its current size next to that appetite, and a warning comes before a breach ([interface.md](interface.md)). Its size cannot be read from self-report, so the body has to ask for indicators. The research proposes some for its own experiment ([proposal](../ai-era-testing/proposal.md)): how long the team takes to diagnose a fault in code it produced, and whether its members, including those who joined later, can say why each major design choice was made. The first reads comprehension and the second intent. The suite above adds a third, comparing the team's step-rejection rate in live sessions against the agent's failure rate on the fixed suite, which reads the people's calibration. Engineering's numbers in Part 2 of the statement already carry the late version of all three: how long changes take, rework, regressions ([value.md](value.md)). A single report can look fine, two years of trend cannot.

## Who mandates and who pays

As [implementations.md](implementations.md) notes at the end of its cost comparison, buying human understanding on purpose now, across product, engineering and QA (supervision time in a method such as building sessions, written intent records, training and keeping the suite of checks), is prevention spend; what that page leaves open is who mandates the conditions and pays the bill.

Nobody below the top will pay it on their own, and the reasons are the incentives every function already has. The product manager gets the feature sooner when the developer hands it to the agent whole. The engineering manager answers for delivery this quarter, and the extra diagnosis time the debt causes is paid next year. The QA lead cannot fund product and engineering hours for human supervision out of a testing budget. The developer who hands over a whole feature is using the tool as it was sold. Each of them is right by their own numbers, and the sum is the loss described above. This is the speed-quality fight of the [problem statement](quality-governance.md) with the same structure: the win visible now on one side, the cost later and elsewhere on the other, and nobody below the top judged on the balance.

The standards put four acts on the governing body, and each lands in the statement ([value.md](value.md)), stating what must hold while leaving the choice of workflow to management ([implementations.md](implementations.md)). The first act, in Part 1, is to name the practice, how the company's software is built with agents, as something it governs; until then nothing in the oversight clause attaches to it. The AI standard puts the scope of automation under the governing body's oversight, to be carried out by skilled, authorized staff after its consequences have been weighed beforehand (38507 5.5).

The second act, in Part 6, sets the conditions on the people who use the agent and the delegation limits on what may be handed to it. The AI standard asks that any human using or responsible for AI understand the AI system being used, hold decision authority or know whom to ask, and be able to intervene (38507 6.2; for a coding agent, intervening requires understanding the software it changes), and that those making decisions have the skills and training their remit demands (6.3). When a developer hands the agent a whole feature, the agent decides things on its own, as in the case where the prompt omitted fraud and the agent invented a rule for it ([implementations.md](implementations.md)), that nobody authorized the developer to decide. The standard for the governance of IT asks the organization to ensure a tool does not exceed its user's authority (38500 7.2.5). What may be handed to the agent in one piece, and what people have to decide, is therefore a delegation limit in Part 6.

The third act, also in Part 6, is the appetite. The risk of losing the knowledge gets its numerical bound like every other kind of risk: how much of it the company accepts, why, and the size of problem above which management escalates ([value.md](value.md)).

The fourth act, as a bet in Part 3, is the money. ISO 37000 lists the conditions under which delegation works, and one of them is that the required resources are available; it adds that nobody can be made to answer for work where they lacked the authority or clear expectations (4.2.2). A body that delegates building software with agents to management and does not pay for the people who keep understanding the software has delegated without meeting that condition, and management cannot be held to account for knowledge it was given no means to keep. The standard for the governance of IT asks the body for assurance that its intentions are translated by personnel with the appropriate competence (38500 5.6.2). It also asks for assurance that the organization has the knowledge and skills to implement change in time (5.8.2). Under agents both assurances have a price, because the competence stops accumulating on its own. The body funds that prevention overhead as a Part 3 bet ([implementations.md](implementations.md) works out the building-session example), and at the review date it puts the indicators above next to the estimates.

The management level has its own standard for AI, ISO/IEC 42001, written for top management and the organization; the governing body is not its addressee. It requires the organization to determine the competence needed by the people whose work affects its AI performance, to ensure they have it, and to keep evidence of it (7.2). It requires that those people know the AI policy and their part in it (7.3). Its reference control for human resources calls for documenting the people and competences used at each stage of an AI system's life, verification included (A.4.6). Its guidance names roles for human oversight among the necessary human resources (B.4.6), asks that the people overseeing the system be informed and trained, and lists human reviewers with authority to override it among the oversight options (B.9.3). Those are the places at the management level where the body's conditions are written down and audited. What is written there is the body's direction: which competence, over which software, held by whom, and paid for from where. Nothing in the management standard tells the body to require comprehension of the company's own software as a competence, and nothing in it will keep that competence if the body does not.

## What not paying costs

The debts' first bill fits the economics research's four categories of quality cost: inspection bought after the fact, rework, and failures that reach customers because nobody could tell right from wrong. The second bill fits none of them. [value.md](value.md) counts a return that comes from the system itself: when the system stays quick and safe to change, every future plan is cheaper to pursue, the next feature ships sooner and the response to a competitor takes weeks. That return is the first thing the company loses when nobody understands the code. The research's cost table puts finding what to rewrite among the most expensive steps once nobody understands the code ([with agents](../ai-era-testing/analysis-agents.md)), so every future change costs more, and every future delay with it. The decision rule on [interface.md](interface.md) has product with engineering work out what postponing a feature costs, case by case. Under comprehension debt that number rises for every feature the company will ever ship, and no incident report will show it. It shows in the numbers engineering already answers for in Part 2 of the statement, how long changes take, rework and regressions ([value.md](value.md)), and the top sees it only when it reads those numbers as a trend.

## Maintain

The research promises in its [purpose statement](quality-governance.md) that organizations gain and maintain clarity on the economics of their quality decisions. The interface keeps the numbers current: bets checked, estimates corrected, the statement refreshed. Once the people who run it can no longer say what a failure would cost or whether a check would catch it, the numbers they correct rest on as little as the evidence behind them. Keeping those people able is the governing body's act inside the statement, in four parts: the practice named as governed in Part 1, the human conditions and delegation limits in Part 6, the appetite for the risk to knowledge in Part 6, and the prevention money in Part 3, checked at review dates against the indicators above. That is what the [problem statement](quality-governance.md) promises under Adoption, AI adoption at a pace the organization can sustain. What each role does with this, the governing body, every department head, product leadership, engineering leadership and QA leadership, is on the [practice page](practice.md).
